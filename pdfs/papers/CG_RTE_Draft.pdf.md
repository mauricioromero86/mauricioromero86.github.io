# The benefit incidence of school vouchers: Evidence from private schools in India [*]

### Mauricio Romero [†] Abhijeet Singh [‡] October 22, 2026

**Abstract**


The incidence of redistributive policies is central to whether they meet their stated goals.
We study this in the context of one of the world’s largest programs to improve social equity
in schooling: a 25% quota in all Indian private schools for students from disadvantaged
groups. We show that, although eligible groups are poorer than the state average, program
benefits accrue disproportionately to better-off households: 7.4% of the program spending
on tuition fees accrues to the bottom socioeconomic quintile, compared to 24.3% to the
top quintile. This is consistent with selection within eligible groups at the application
stage: program applicants are disproportionately drawn from more-educated and better-off
households. Lottery-based estimates of the causal effect of receiving a quota seat show that
three-quarters of lottery-winners would have attended a private school even without the
voucher; the bulk of program spending is inframarginal for school choice. Finally, we use
rich survey data to show that low application rates for poorer children reflect information
constraints and application frictions. A randomized intervention providing information and
application assistance substantially raises application rates but has similar estimated effects
across socioeconomic groups. Our results illustrate how constraints facing potential applicants
can make redistributive policies regressive in practice.

## **1 Introduction**

Governments routinely support disadvantaged citizens in accessing essential services

through targeted vouchers. However, many eligible individuals do not apply for
social benefits (Currie, 2006), and the _de_ _facto_ incidence of program benefits may differ

substantially from the program’s intent. This may, in turn, dampen downstream effects

on the intended policy outcome, namely expanding choice for poorer citizens in key

sectors such as housing, healthcare, and education. Despite substantial research on

interventions to improve application rates (Ko and Moffitt, 2024; Banerjee et al., 2024),

the empirical importance of this link between benefit incidence, program effectiveness,

and various application constraints remains unclear. However, it may be central to

concerns of both redistribution and program effectiveness.


We study this link directly in the context of the world’s largest school voucher policy,

which provides targeted vouchers to attend private schools in India. Targeted school

voucher programs are common worldwide (Urquiola, 2016) and resemble similar

interventions in other domains. The Right to Education (RTE) Act, 2009, mandated a

25% quota in the student intake of all private schools for students from disadvantaged

economic and/or caste backgrounds to reduce socioeconomic stratification. The state

pays tuition fees (up to a cap) for students enrolled under this quota and schools

are not allowed to charge top-up fees. In 2018/19, approximately 4 million students

nationwide used a quota seat to attend private schools (Indus Action, 2019), which

are common in India and, as in many countries, differentiated in price, quality, and

amenities (Muralidharan and Sundararaman, 2015; Andrabi et al., 2025).


We study this policy in Chhattisgarh, a state with a population of _∼_ 29 million that

has implemented it since 2010. Eligible students in the state can apply for admission

under the policy in either preschool grades (Nursery and Kindergarten) or in Grade
1, the formal start of primary schooling, depending on their age. [1] Program eligibility

is determined by the age of the child and official documentation of household

disadvantage, either on grounds of being poor (“economically weaker sections”) or

from a disadvantaged population group. Applications for the voucher are made online

and seats are allocated using a centralized algorithm with lottery-based allocation of

oversubscribed slots (Abdulkadiro˘glu et al., 2017). We evaluate the benefit incidence

of the policy and its effects on voucher recipients using administrative data on all

eligible applications in 2019, primary survey data on voucher applicants, and a field

experiment that relaxes application constraints for treated households.


We begin our analysis by computing the _de_ _facto_ incidence of policy benefits. We

collected primary survey data on the socioeconomic characteristics of voucher applicants

and mapped them to representative population-wide surveys. Using this, we show

more likely to apply - voucher applicants have significantly higher average parental

education and household assets than both eligible groups and the general population.


Next, we use the lottery-based allocation of oversubscribed slots to estimate the effects

of receiving a free seat on students’ enrollment choices. Being allotted an RTE quota seat

increases the probability of attending any private school by 25 percentage points; virtually
all lottery-winning students attend private institutions, but so do _∼_ 78% of applicants who
were _not_ assigned an RTE seat; the latter enroll as non-quota students who pay regular

school fees. The extensive margin effect is concentrated in preschool grades, shifting some

students from home care to preschool. By Grade 1, when enrollment is compulsory and
near-universal, this effect is 13 percentage points. [2] Lottery winners attend schools that

are, on average, more expensive and offer English-medium instruction, but much of the

voucher spending is inframarginal for school choice - as an extreme, 54% of applicants

who lost the lottery nevertheless attend their top-ranked school by paying full price (i.e.,

full inframarginality). These results are directly linked to regressive selection: private
school attendance in representative state-level data rises sharply from _∼_ 5% in the bottom
decile to _∼_ 64% in the top decile. Thus, the over-representation of wealthier households
in the applicant pool raises distributional concerns _and_ reduces the policy’s effectiveness.


These muted effects are striking for a policy intended to substantially expand school

choice for students from disadvantaged backgrounds. It is unsurprising that wealthier

households, who would otherwise send their children to private schools, apply for free

seats when eligible. The puzzle is why poorer households, who are more likely to be

eligible for these seats, do not apply in greater numbers. In the third and final part of

our analysis, we focus on barriers to application that may explain this regressive take-up.

central to identifying appropriate interventions to boost progressivity.

## **2 Policy Background and Context**

### **2.1 Private schooling in India**


Fee-charging private schools in India account for _∼_ 31% of primary school enrollment
in rural areas and _∼_ 50% in urban areas (Pratham, 2019; Kingdon, 2020). At the

primary level, private schools typically have higher enrollment and more teachers than

government schools. They are also more likely to include an integrated preschool

section, use English as a medium of instruction, and offer single-grade classrooms

and subject-specific teachers. Teachers in private schools are typically younger, less

likely to have completed formal teacher training, and are paid substantially less

than teachers in government schools. However, they are less likely to be absent

from school or engaged in non-teaching activities. Kingdon (2020) provides a

review of the private schooling sector in India.


Evidence on relative differences in quality is scarcer. Cross-sectionally, students in private

schools score higher than those in public schools, but this difference is confounded by

selection: private school students are more likely to be from higher SES households,

urban, and male, and likely also selected on unobserved characteristics. Muralidharan

and Sundararaman (2015) provide the best estimates of the causal effects of attending a

private school in rural Andhra Pradesh and find that there are large effects on English and

the national language (Hindi) with no evidence of effects in math and the local language;

similar results are shown by Singh (2015) using value-added models in the same state.

More recently, Berg et al. (2025) show in rural Tamil Nadu, a neighboring state, that while

private and government schools have similar average test score value-added in math

and lower in Tamil in primary school grades, there is a stark productivity difference at

pre-primary ages: private preschools outperform government-run institutions by about
0.6–0.7 _σ_ in both math and local language. [10] These results refer only to average differences

across sectors. There is no causal evidence on the distribution of school-level productivity
_within_ the private and public sectors in India. The closest evidence from a similar setting

is provided by Andrabi et al. (2025) from three districts in rural Punjab in Pakistan. They

find substantial heterogeneity in schools’ within-sector productivity. Private schools

have a higher average value-added, but there is substantial overlap across sectors.

### **2.3 Quotas in Chhattisgarh: context and lottery design**

Our study is based in Chhattisgarh state, which had a population of _∼_ 29.4 million
in 2020. _∼_ 40% of the population was estimated to be below the poverty line in 2011
(compared to _∼_ 22% nationally) and the state ranked 21st (out of 28) in its achievements

of the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals in 2019 (NITI Aayog, 2020).


Chhattisgarh has implemented the RTE quota since 2010. The government reimburses

school fees for students admitted under the quota up to an INR 7,000 cap and

provides student grants for books and uniforms. Schools cannot charge top-up fees

(even if the school fees exceed the reimbursement cap).


Eligibility for the program combines two conditions. The first relates to students’

age: students must be aged between 3–4 years to apply for admission in Nursery,

4–5 years for admission in kindergarten (KG), and 5–6.5 years for admission in

Grade 1. The second requirement is to provide official documentation of household

disadvantage, either on the basis of being income-poor (“economically weaker sections”)

or from a historically disadvantaged population group (“disadvantaged group”). In

Chhattisgarh, demonstrating economic disadvantage requires having access to a card
certifying “Below Poverty Line” status as determined in administrative surveys. [15]


Documenting membership of a “disadvantaged group” requires showing “caste

certificates” issued by the state government that certify the household as belonging
to Scheduled Castes (SC) or Scheduled Tribes (ST). [16]


Applications for the program are open for roughly four weeks in February–April of

each year - the exact dates vary across years - for enrollment in the subsequent

school year, starting in July. These applications are made through a centralized

application portal that shows parents which schools have seats available (based on

where they live) and requires them to submit a rank-ordered list. Parents can do this

on home devices or at internet cafes and government-authorized “choice centers”,

which are effectively private internet cafes registered and trained to help citizens

apply for benefits through government websites for a fee.

### **3.1 Statewide data on RTE applications in 2019**

Our analysis of RTE applications focuses on the 2019 application cycle. We use data

from three sources: (i) application data provided by parents in 2019; (ii) two rounds of

phone-based survey data collected by the research team to study enrollment; and (iii)

administrative data on school characteristics. We describe each of these sources below.


**3.1.1** **Administrative** **data** **on** **applications**


We obtained data for all eligible applications submitted in 2019 through the online

allocation system to implement the RTE in Chhattisgarh. The data include parents’ school

rankings, the assigned school (if any), and limited household characteristics, including

phone numbers. In addition to data on students’ applications, we also received data

on the number of seats available in each grade at each private school registered in the

administrative system. Parents applied to schools in March–April 2019 and were notified

of the school assignment in May. Enrollment decisions were finalized by July 2019.


In 2019, valid applications were received from 54,676 eligible students, 7,217 of whom

were not allotted an RTE seat in the first round (see Table A.1; Panel A). Nearly half

(48%) of the applicants were female and 56% lived in a rural area. More than 50%

of applicants have only one school on their preference list, and 92% have at most
three preferences. [19] There were 5,863 schools in the lottery, with an average of 10

seats available but with 15 students applying. Schools are more likely to have seats

available in Nursery than in Grade 1 (see Table A.1, Panel C).

**3.1.2** **Primary** **data** **from** **phone** **surveys**


All applicants were required to provide a phone number to apply. [20] We used these

phone numbers to survey parents on the household’s schooling choices, socioeconomic

background, and educational inputs. Data from these phone surveys are central to

our analysis of incidence and the effects of winning the lottery.


First, between August and September of 2020, we attempted to call all individuals

(N=16,703) with lottery-based variation in whether they received an RTE offer. We

collected information about which school the applicant eventually enrolled in for

the 2019–20 school year, along with basic school characteristics (e.g., medium of

instruction and fee level) and household characteristics (parental education and

occupation). We made up to five attempts to reach each household and completed

interviews with about 44% of the targeted households.


Between November 2020 and January 2021, we conducted a further round of phone

surveys to collect detailed information on household assets, which we use to compare

the socioeconomic status of applicants to that of the eligible population. We attempted to

recontact all households interviewed in the first phone survey and completed interviews

with 59% of them. Further, since the original phone survey sample only focused on

applicants with lottery-based variation, we also attempted to interview a random sample

of 1,203 applicants from the non-lottery sample (who had a probability of one of being

assigned to a private school). Of these 1,203 students, 462 completed our phone survey.

### **3.2 Representative data on the statewide population**


Understanding the incidence of quota benefits requires representative data on eligible

students, not just those who applied. We use population-level data from three sources.


**3.2.1** **National** **Family** **Health** **Survey** **2019–21**


To establish a representative benchmark for the eligible population, we use

household-level microdata from the fifth round of the National Family Health

we can estimate the policy’s incidence.

## **4 Quantifying the incidence of program benefits**


The first part of our analysis aims to directly quantify the progressivity of benefit
incidence in practice. [25] We focus on assessing three channels that may determine the
benefit incidence of the RTE quotas. The first is _policy targeting_ : by design, the population

groups eligible for the program may be more deprived than the overall population.

This was the intention of the RTE quotas, but the effectiveness/sensitivity of the specific

indicators used to identify disadvantage remains an empirical matter. The second is
_selection_ _in_ _policy_ _take-up:_ applicants self-select out of eligible groups and may be richer
or poorer than the eligible population or the general population. The third is _selection_
_in_ _benefit_ _allocation:_ even conditional on applying, the allocation process may change the

incidence of benefits if application patterns differ substantially across economic groups

(e.g., if richer households apply to more schools or to more expensive schools).


Table 4 and Figure 1 compare all quota applicants in our phone survey to households
in the statewide NFHS with at least one child aged between 4–7 years. [26] We

compare three groups: (a) all NFHS households with a child of eligible age, (b)

NFHS households which are likely to be eligible for RTE seats, and (c) the actual

population of applicants for quota seats.


The first relevant pattern, comparing Columns 1 and 2, is that households likely to

be eligible - defined here as either belonging to Scheduled Castes/Tribes or below

durables - have fewer assets, with parents less likely to have completed secondary
schooling, than the overall population. Thus, at least the _intent_ of the policy to be

redistributive seems borne out in the choice of eligibility criteria: targeted groups

are, in fact, more disadvantaged than the overall population.


Second, however, actual applicants are substantially more advantaged than the

population of eligible households or, indeed, the average for all households in the

state with children of eligible ages. In Figures 1 and A.2, we confirm this across the

full distribution of socioeconomic status, as measured by the asset index: the eligible

population is poorer than the statewide population, which in turn is more deprived

## **5 Effects of winning the quota seat on school choice**


The regressive incidence documented above is potentially important not only for

distributional concerns but also because the effects of receiving a free seat may

differ by income. A key premise of the policy was that poorer households are

financially constrained and thus cannot attend private schools; however, if most
actual quota beneficiaries tend to be from upper-income households, who _can_ attend

private schools by paying regular fees, it is possible that receiving a free seat does

not relax any constraints for private school attendance.


We investigate this using lotteries embedded in the allocation mechanism. These

lotteries only provide identification for students who applied to oversubscribed schools.

Therefore, our analysis proceeds in three steps. First, we estimate the causal effect

of receiving a free seat in our oversubscribed sample. Second, we examine whether

treatment effects vary by the ex-ante probability of winning a seat, exploiting the fact that

applicants who were just below the margin of facing lottery risk should be informative

about the counterfactual for deterministic applicants. Third, we use both the reweighting

procedure proposed by Angrist and Fern´andez-Val (2010) and generalized random

forests (Wager and Athey, 2018; Athey et al., 2019) to extrapolate treatment effects from

the lottery sample to the deterministic sample based on observable characteristics.

### **5.2 Effects on school choice in the lottery sample**


**5.2.1** **Extensive** **margin** **effects** **on** **school** **choice**


We classify students by whether they were (a) enrolled in a private preschool or school,

(b) enrolled in a government school, and (c) not enrolled — and study the effects of being
offered an RTE seat on each of these margins separately for the 2019–20 academic year. [34]


Nearly all applicants who were assigned an RTE seat are enrolled in private schooling;
however, so are three-quarters of compliers who _did not_ receive a free seat (Table 6). The

estimated treatment effect on the probability of attending a private school is only around

25 percentage points. This increase is concentrated among students who had applied for

a seat in preschool grades, with a substantial movement from non-enrollment to private
schooling. [35] Among applicants for admission in Grade 1 — of whom 98% of lottery losers

are formally enrolled - the effect on private schooling is only 13 percentage points.

eligible students would not be able to access private schools without the quota. It

is also modest compared to context-specific empirical benchmarks. Muralidharan

and Sundararaman (2015) show that providing (untargeted) vouchers to students
in public schools boosted private sector enrollment by _∼_ 50 percentage points in

### **5.3 Generalizing estimates to non-lottery applicants**


Our main estimates rely on lotteries in oversubscribed schools, which raises a natural

question regarding external validity: are the effects of the quota different for applicants to

undersubscribed schools? This distinction is potentially important, as approximately 69%

of applicants in our sample applied to schools with no lottery risk (i.e., “deterministic”

applicants). If the counterfactual for deterministic applicants is markedly different (e.g.,

none would attend private schools in the absence of the RTE quota), the implications

for the policy’s success in expanding school choice and integration, and for its incidence,

would be very different. We investigate this in multiple ways.


Our first exercise examines whether estimated treatment effects vary by the ex-ante

probability of winning an RTE seat. The intuition for this exercise is simple: given

some uncertainty in the exact number of applicants/seats at the time of application,

examining the counterfactual for applicants who were just below the margin of

facing lottery risk (say, where the probability of an offer was 90%) should also

be informative about the counterfactual for deterministic applicants who faced no

lottery risk (i.e., a 100% probability of getting an offer).


Figure 3 plots the effect of receiving an RTE seat on private school enrollment across

different bins of the probability of winning the lottery. Control means are high across the

distribution. If we focus on compliers (Figure 3b), the control mean is slightly lower for

marginally oversubscribed schools, but remains above 67%. Except for very competitive

schools (those with a probability of winning the lottery under 30%), the treatment effects

are similar and we cannot reject that they are equal (for the ITT the p-value of equality

is 0.800, for the LATE the p-value of equality is 0.127). Even under the most conservative
counterfactual for deterministic applicants, this would imply that _∼_ 67% would attend a

private school in the absence of the lottery. Likewise, if we focus on Grade 1 applicants,

over 40% of lottery losers (as well as compliers who lose the lottery) still attend their

top choice (see Figure A.5) regardless of how oversubscribed the school is, and there is


exposure to peers from families that can afford higher fees. However, because around half of compliers
among lottery losers attend the same school and pay full fees, the net change in peer composition is limited
for a substantial share of applicants.

Our second exercise uses the re-weighting procedure proposed by Angrist and

All three exercises suggest that most applicants, even deterministic ones, would attend

## **6 Investigating barriers to application**


Barriers to application appear to be key to understanding who applies for, and thereby

who is allotted, the quota seats. This section investigates these concerns.

### **6.1 Survey evidence on information and application constraints**


The simplest explanation for undersubscription is that many individuals do not know
about the program or find it too complex to apply. [43] If these constraints affect poorer

households more, that could also explain the regressive selection into the applicant pool.


Survey data from in-person interviews in Raipur district, collected from a sample

identified using comprehensive household listings of sampled neighborhoods and

villages, suggest such constraints are important in this setting (Figure 5). First,

information constraints appear both large and regressive. There is a stark SES gradient

in whether households, with children between 3–7 years, have even heard of the policy:

65% of parents in the top decile have heard of the policy, while only 20% of parents in

the bottom decile report the same. This result is robust to focusing on heterogeneity

within rural/urban areas, and focusing on heterogeneity within each sampling unit

(i.e., small geographical areas) - see Table A.7 and Figure A.7.


Second, potential frictions to submitting applications also burden poorer households

differentially. Nearly all (98%) high socioeconomic status households report access to the

internet at home, but only 9.1% of households in the bottom decile do so (see Figure 5).

This differentially burdens poorer households from accessing the centralized application

portal. Fewer low-SES households report having ever applied for government benefits

online, and fewer of them can access support from others for doing so.

**6.2.1** **Intervention** **and** **Experiment** **Design**


Our intervention aimed to address the twin constraints facing households: insufficient

information about the policy and the lack of means to apply through online portals.

We focus on both constraints, given evidence from other sectors and countries that

information provision alone may not suffice to raise take-up when households also face

other barriers (Banerjee et al., 2021; Finkelstein and Notowidigdo, 2019).


We used our in-person household survey conducted in February 2022 as the baseline

and restricted our analysis to households that reported having a mobile phone (N=914,

out of 1,059 households in total). This sample was not selected based on quota eligibility
(or predictors thereof). We randomized _∼_ 50% of households in each locality (N=459) to

receive the intervention. Observed characteristics (including previous knowledge of the

RTE policy) are balanced between the treatment and the control group (see Table 8).


The intervention consisted of providing detailed information about the policy and, if the

household was willing to apply and eligible, assisting them in applying. The intervention

was implemented between April 15 and May 2, 2022, when quota applications were

open, and consisted of the following, sequential, steps:


1. Call all treatment households to offer information and potential support to apply

for a quota seat (if eligible). If we could not reach households at the phone number

they provided, we revisited them to obtain this information.

2. Households were asked if they had heard about the RTE quota, if they knew that

the applications were currently open, if they had already thought of applying to the

program, and if they were interested in receiving more information.

3. Interested households were provided with detailed information on the eligibility

criteria for the policy and the documents required for demonstrating eligibility.

Surveyors collected information on each document the household reported having.

4. If a household reported having all required documents, we offered application

support and scheduled a visit. If a household lacked all required documents, we

provided information on where they could obtain them. We provided applicants

with a number to call if they obtained these documents and needed further support.

5. For interested households (which reported having all documents), an interviewer

## **7 Discussion**


Motivated by the goal of expanding educational access for disadvantaged students,

quotas in Indian private schools constitute the largest voucher program in the

world and one of the most ambitious attempts to reduce socioeconomic stratification

in schooling in any setting. We evaluated whether the policy, as implemented

in Chhattisgarh, delivers on that promise.


Our results paint a complex picture. The policy yields substantial monetary gains for

applicants who receive a free place. Getting a seat leads some eligible students to

attend preschool and others to attend schools they otherwise could not afford. Yet, this

success is qualified. The quota is used primarily by households that would send their

children to private schools anyway, and much of the expenditure is inframarginal to

school choice. Thus, the policy largely serves as a monetary transfer for beneficiaries

without achieving the goal of changing classroom composition by admitting financially

constrained disadvantaged students. These insights are likely informative about the RTE

policy across India: RTE quota seats are undersubscribed across states, and extensive
margin effects (i.e., expanding access to private schools) appear to be modest at best. [53]


We show that these (muted) effects on school choice are consistent with selection

into applications: poorer students are less likely to apply, even when eligible. This

reflects several constraints - information, application complexity, and documentation

- that disproportionately affect poorer households, rather than a lack of parental

demand. There may be substantial scope to improve policy effectiveness, even under

current policy rules, by addressing these constraints. However, potential interventions

to address regressive selection are likely to differ in design from those focused only

on improving application rates: in our setting, providing information and application

assistance boosted application rates by 43%, with similar estimated effects across

socioeconomic groups. This plausibly reflects two distinct features of our intervention:

we addressed only a subset of constraints (e.g., leaving documentation unsolved),

The incidence or inframarginality of program benefits has not been a principal concern

in this literature but it is likely that regressivity also affects program effectiveness

elsewhere. For instance, Angrist et al. (2002), evaluating the PACES voucher scheme in

Colombia, find similarly modest treatment effects on the extensive margin of private
school enrollment ( _∼_ 15%). This program required applicants to seek and secure

admission to a private school before applying; if wealthier households are more likely

to satisfy this requirement, this plausibly produces similar regressivity within the

population of eligible households as we document in India. More generally, there

is substantial evidence that information frictions and application complexity deter

applications across a range of educational settings, and that addressing these factors

increases application rates (see, e.g., Bettinger et al. (2012); Dynarski et al. (2021); Agte

rates but also change the _composition_ of program applicants and beneficiaries: this

selection raises important distributional concerns and reduces the average effectiveness

of interventions in expanding access to educational opportunities. Plausibly, a careful

analysis of their benefit incidence may also affect policy support for publicly funded

voucher programs, especially where they compete directly with resources for public

education (which recent evidence suggests is strongly progressive in its incidence and

in downstream returns through the labor market (Gethin, 2025b,a)).


Finally, the issues of program design and take-up highlighted by our results

apply more broadly to social programs, even outside education. Information

constraints and application complexity deter applications across programs, and the

documentation constraint also applies broadly. These constraints may jointly induce

similar regressivity in benefit incidence and substantial inframarginality in program

spending. Targeting household deprivation more precisely, rather than through coarser

group-level proxies with self-selection, is often beyond the administrative capacity

of governments. Thus, delivering supplementary interventions to households to

lower application barriers is often the feasible second-best solution. The optimal

design of such interventions - both in terms of bundling program components

and further targeting - to improve progressivity and not just application rates

remains an important area for further investigation.

**May** **2019:** **Lottery** **Allocation** **and** **Notification**


- Centralized allocation using Immediate Acceptance algorithm.

- Enrollment decisions finalized by July 2019.


**NFHS-5** **(2019–2021):** **National** **Family** **Health** **Survey**


- State-representative household survey used to benchmark SES distribution of eligible
population.

- _Variables_ : asset ownership, housing characteristics _→_ PCA-based wealth index.


**Aug–Sep** **2020:** **Phone** **Survey** **1** **(Lottery** **Sample)**

- Attempted to call all **N** **=** **16,703** applicants with lottery-based variation.

- Up to 5 call attempts per household; **44%** **response** **rate** ( **7,376** interviews).

- _Variables_ : school enrollment (2019–20), school characteristics (medium of instruction,
fees), parental education and occupation.


**Nov** **2020–Jan** **2021:** **Phone** **Survey** **2** **(Assets** **and** **Deterministic** **Sample)**


- Recontacted Survey 1 respondents: **59%** **response** **rate** ( **4,338** interviews).

- Additionally sampled **1,203** applicants from non-lottery (deterministic) subsample;
**462** **responded** (38%).

- _Variables_ : detailed household assets _→_ SES index for incidence analysis.


**Feb** **2022:** **In-Person** **Household** **Survey** **(Raipur** **District)**


- Representative sample from household listings in urban and rural Raipur.

- **N** **=** **1,059** households with children aged 3–7.

- _Variables_ : SES (assets, parental education), stated choice experiment (demand for private
schools), knowledge of RTE policy, internet access, experience with online applications.


**Apr** **15–May** **2,** **2022:** **Randomized** **Intervention** **(Raipur** **District)**


- Sample: **N** **=** **914** households with mobile phone (from Feb 2022 survey).

- **N** **=** **459** randomized to treatment (information + application assistance).

- _Outcomes_ : knowledge of RTE, applied to RTE, secured/used RTE seat.

## **B Incidence in the household data from Raipur**


We confirm the main findings from Section 4 using our in-person household survey
in Raipur district (N=1,059 households with children aged 3–7). This sample was
drawn from comprehensive household listings in sampled neighborhoods and villages,
independently of the RTE application process, and is thus informative about the broader
population of eligible households (including those who never applied).


Figure B.1 shows the SES gradient in awareness and application from the full sample.
The proportion of households that have heard of the RTE policy rises sharply with
socioeconomic status, from below 20% in the bottom decile to above 65% at the top.
Application rates follow a similar gradient: fewer than 7.1% of households in the lowest
decile report having applied, compared with over 16% at the top. This pattern mirrors
the regressive selection documented in the statewide applicant data.

Application frictions, rather than weak targeting, drive the regressive incidence. Figure
B.2, akin to Figure 2 for the in-person sample, shows the incidence of applications and
admissions in our Raipur household sample. The observed distribution of applicants and
admitted students is skewed towards the top quintiles of the socioeconomic distribution

whereas the bottom quintile contributes approximately 10%.


If every eligible child already enrolled in a private school applied for and used an RTE
seat (Figure B.2b), the incidence barely changes relative to the status quo - this reflects
that the likelihood of sending a child to private school is correlated with socioeconomic
status. If, instead, all eligible households apply and enroll their children under the RTE
quota (Figure B.2c), the distribution reverses: the bottom quintile would account for
over 30% of beneficiaries, while the top quintile would contribute less than 10%. This
confirms that the eligibility criteria are, by design, progressive. The regressive incidence
documented in Section 4 is consistent with differential take-up across the socioeconomic
distribution, rather than targeting design being the principal driver.

## **C Caste and income eligibility: joint incidence**


This appendix addresses how caste-based and income-based eligibility for the RTE
25% quota interact in practice. The analysis partially addresses the concern that,
although regressive in income, the policy would still achieve its primary goal of
increasing the representation of Scheduled Castes and Tribes in private schools, even
if it disproportionately benefits wealthier individuals within this group. Section F
already highlighted that, although spatial constraints limit the equalization of caste
composition, substantial reductions in segregation remain possible. We further study
this along the income dimension, asking both how representative Scheduled Caste and
Tribe applicants are of the Scheduled Caste and Tribe populations and how the two
eligibility tracks jointly shape the applicant pool (see Table C.1).

## **D Demand for private schools from low-SES households**


Regressive selection is inevitable if too few poor households wish to send their children to
private schools, even for free. [55] Similarly, there is limited scope to reduce inframarginality
if only a few households that prefer private schools are financially constrained.


To understand preferences over different schools, we adapted the methodology
of Delavande and Zafar (2019) to elicit parental preferences for private schooling
across the socioeconomic distribution. Specifically, we provided parents with five
(fictitious) schools - two public and three private, representing the range of choices
in similar markets - from which they must choose where to enroll their child. We
presented characteristics for five schools to each household:


1. **Nearby** **public** **school:** Enrollment, number of teachers/classrooms, and highest
grade were fixed near the 25th percentile of school enrollment in the public sector.


2. **Distant** **public** **school:** Enrollment, number of teachers/classrooms and highest
grade were fixed near the 75th percentile of school enrollment in the public sector.


3. **Lower-range** **private** **school:** Enrollment, number of teachers/classrooms and
highest grade in the school benchmarked to the bottom tercile of private school fees.


4. **Mid-range** **private** **school:** Enrollment, number of teachers/classrooms and highest
grade in the school benchmarked to the middle tercile of private school fees.


5. **Expensive** **private** **school:** Enrollment, number of teachers/classrooms and highest
grade in the school benchmarked to the top tercile of private school fees.


We randomized the distance from each school to the household: for School 1, this
was randomized to be between 250 and 750 meters in increments of 250 meters;
for all other schools, this was randomized to be between 500 and 2000 meters in
increments of 500 meters. Reflecting actual prevalence, the medium of instruction
was fixed as Hindi for Schools 1-3 and English for School 5, and randomized between
Hindi and English for School 4. The precise school fees shown to households for
each school were randomly selected from a range of values. This was created using
the p10-p90 spread in each tercile, with INR 1,000 added to and subtracted from the
boundaries to ensure overlap in fee distributions across schools.


The specific values for each characteristic were computed separately for rural and urban
areas in Raipur and are presented in Table D.1. These characteristics were presented
to households using a visual stimulus (see Figure D.1) and a surveyor explained
each of the characteristics in detail before eliciting responses.

## **E Extrapolating treatment effects to deterministic** **applicants**

We extrapolate LATE estimates to deterministic applicants using the reweighting
procedure of Angrist and Fern´andez-Val (2010). The approach reweights covariate-specific
LATEs from the lottery sample to match the covariate distribution in the deterministic
sample, assuming that treatment effects are homogeneous conditional on observables.
Covariates include caste (SC, ST, OBC, or other), rural residence, an indicator for
listing more than one school, and above-median indicators of public school availability,
private school density, Scheduled Caste and Tribe population shares, and poverty
rate - each interacted with application grade.


We assess the reliability of this extrapolation using the split-sample procedure (`a la
Bisbee et al. (2017)). In each of 1,000 simulations, we randomly select half of the
experimental strata, estimate the LATE on that half, and extrapolate to the remaining
strata using the reweighting procedure. We then compare the extrapolated LATE to
the directly estimated LATE in the hold-out strata (Figure E.1). The distribution of
directly estimated LATEs across subsamples is tight (SD = 0.015, with a mean of 0.24),
consistent with limited heterogeneity across strata (Figure E.1a). Bias is low: 83.5% of
predictions fall within 5 percentage points of the target LATE (Figure E.1c). However, the
correlation between targeted and predicted LATEs is negative (Figure E.1b), indicating
that the procedure does not predict which strata have higher or lower effects. We
interpret this combination - low bias but no predictive power at the stratum level

- as consistent with limited treatment-effect heterogeneity: the overall extrapolated
effect is reliable, but stratum-level variation is too small to target.

## **F Spatial supply-side constraints to quota take-up**


Our analysis primarily examined why households do not apply for quota seats, even
when private schools are available. This focus was appropriate because information
constraints and application hurdles are relevant margins for policy action to improve
take-up. Overall, however, a further constraint on improving school integration
is whether private schools are located in communities with high proportions of
disadvantaged households. If most eligible households have few private schools
available in their communities, the policy would be limited in the ability to reduce
stratification (see, e.g., Monarrez (2022); Campos and Kearns (2022)).


73


We study the extent to which such spatial constraints might matter. To this end,
we assembled geolocated population and poverty rate data for individual villages
and towns (Asher et al., 2021), along with GPS locations of recognized schools and
administrative data on school characteristics. [58] Private schools are common in rural
and urban areas, but not universal. They are less likely to be available in communities
with a higher proportion of eligible disadvantaged groups (Figure F.1). In the state
overall, 43.4% of Grade 1 enrollment is in communities with only public schools; this
figure is 53.2% for Scheduled Caste and Tribe students and 55.1% for income-poor
students. [59] Thus, the spatial distribution of private schools and disadvantaged groups
is a relevant constraint for explaining statewide differences in the probability of private
school enrollment for disadvantaged and non-disadvantaged households. While the
proportion of students from Scheduled Castes and Tribes is 28.0 percentage points
higher in public schools state-wide, 60% of this difference (17 percentage points)
is within communities (see Table F.1), thus substantial segregation remains within
communities with both private and public schools.

## **G Applications, allocation, and schooling responses**

In Section 5.3, we showed that the average response to an offer is roughly the same
across all values of the lottery risk in the data. In this Appendix, we sketch a model
and conditions under which the composition of applicants is roughly the same across
lottery-risk levels, which is consistent with this finding.

### **G.1 Setup**

We assume there are N households of mass 1 with one child each. School fees,
quality, and spots are fixed. All households can apply for a voucher to a private
school _j_ . All households can choose to send their children to public school,
to private school, or not to school.


We let _θ_ describe household type (which includes preferences, taste shocks, and
socioeconomic characteristics). We let _Bθ_ _≥_ 0 describe the gain in optimized utility
from receiving a voucher, and we let _gθ_ be the probability that a household of type _θ_
can apply (that they are eligible, aware of the program, have the correct documents,
etc.). [62] A household will apply if the cost of applying _C ≤_ _qBθ_, where _q_ is the probability
that the household will be offered a voucher. Because the lottery has no priorities,
all households applying to the same school face the same _q_ .


Conditional on type, costs are drawn from
